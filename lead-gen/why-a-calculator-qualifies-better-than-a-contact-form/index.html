The calculator as lead magnet: why a heat-pump cost tool pre-qualifies better than a contact form

A contact form asks one question: are you a person with a phone number? A calculator asks four that matter — what system, what size, what floor, where — and answers something in return. That exchange is the whole reason we build tools instead of forms. Across the EPC lead-gen sites APX runs, the calculator path is what produced the 780+ leads we've routed to assessors and installers (internal APX aggregate, September 2026).

Here is what the two real tools in our build folder actually ask, measured 3 September 2026, and why each question is a filter.

Tool one: the underfloor heating cost calculator

The UFH tool (/Users/jamiemac/Projects/UFH Cost/index.html, 16,284 bytes) is a three-step funnel. The step indicator is explicit: "Step 1 of 3: System Type", then "Coverage Area", then "Contact Details".

Step 1 — which system. Four buttons: Wet UFH (Overlay / Screed), Floor Milling UFH, Electric Mat UFH, Not Sure / Need Advice. This is the qualification. A "wet overlay" click means a screed or board system on a floor being rebuilt or poured — a real project with a real budget. "Electric mat" is usually one bathroom. "Not sure" is a different sales conversation, and the lead email carries that field so nobody phones the wrong conversation.

Step 2 — how big and on what. A scope select with m² ranges printed on the options: Single Room / Extension (10–30 m²), Ground Floor Only (40–90 m²), Whole House Retrofit (100+ m²), New Build. Then subfloor: Concrete, Timber Joists, or Anhydrite Screed. Scope converts a looker from a project: nobody types "100+ m² whole-house retrofit" on a Tuesday afternoon for fun. The subfloor question also filters impossible quotes — milling into an existing concrete slab is a different job to laying mats on timber joists, and the tool forces the choice before contact details are even asked for.

Step 3 — where and who. Postcode (labelled "For local pricing") and full name. That's it. No email, no phone.

Then the conversion is the clever part: on submit the tool fires a GA4 generate_lead event with the chosen system as `lead_type`, then opens WhatsApp at the business number with a pre-filled message containing every answer — UFH type, scope, subfloor, postcode, name. The lead arrives in the installer's phone already formatted as a quote brief. There is no "enquiry received, we'll be in touch" black hole.

Tool two: the EPC pre-assessment

The other build (/Users/jamiemac/Projects/epc-pre-assessment/index.html, 29,454 bytes) runs a rules engine in `script.js` against five required fields: postcode, property type (House / Flat / HMO), tenure (private rented / owner occupied / social housing), current EPC band (A–F or Unknown), and benefit status. Then it computes an answer:

  • Flats and HMOs get ventilation on the likely-measures list; heat pumps are dropped for flats (you generally can't put one on a second-floor flat).
  • Benefit households see a £4,000–£15,000 funding estimate; others see £2,000–£8,000.
  • Postcodes starting with B (Birmingham) add solar PV to the measures list.
  • Target band is C for benefit households, B otherwise — the escalation logic from the numbers below.

Each of those five fields is a filter with a business reason. Tenure separates landlords (who have a legal deadline) from owner-occupiers (who have a comfort bill). Current EPC band separates "probably fundable now" from "already compliant". Benefit status is the ECO-style funding gate. A contact form gets you a name; this gets you a segment.

Why the numbers behind the questions matter

For a calculator to pre-qualify, its outputs have to be real. Both tools anchor to verified public scheme figures. From GOV.UK's Boiler Upgrade Scheme page, fetched 3 September 2026: £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump, £5,000 towards a biomass boiler, £2,500 towards an air-to-air heat pump, plus an extra £1,500 for off-gas-oil properties until March 2027. From the MEES landlord guidance (same access date): F/G-rated domestic private rentals cannot be let without a registered exemption since 1 April 2020, the landlord cost cap is £3,500 including VAT, and government's stated trajectory is EPC band C for as many privately rented homes as possible by 2030, following a 2026 consultation.

Those two facts change the shape of the leads. A private landlord with an EPC E is a different buyer to a benefit household in a D-terrace, and the tools know it before a human does.
