Made Smarter West Midlands, explained for a 5 to 50 person manufacturer

If you run a machine shop, fabricator or engineering firm in the West Midlands and you've heard "Made Smarter" but not read a plain-English version of what it actually is; this is it. The short version: it's grant money, up to £20,000 at a 50% match, for digital technology and the specialist advice to implement it. That's the figure on the official programme pages (madesmarter.uk and businessgrowthwestmidlands.org.uk, both accessed 13 September 2026). What follows is what those pages don't tell you in one read: what the money is for, the honest catch, and how a digital project actually gets structured so it's fundable.

First, what the grant is and isn't

Made Smarter West Midlands is 50% match funding with a maximum of £20,000 per company, available for digital technology equipment and/or specialist advice to implement a project. "Match funding" means you pay the other 50%; a £40,000 project costs you £20,000. Two figures worth holding onto:

  • Up to £20,000 is the maximum grant. The cap is the grant, not the project; you can spend more than £40k and fund the eligible portion.
  • It's not a loan and it's not a tax break. It's cash toward eligible spend, and it's paid in arrears against milestones; you fund the work first and claim back.

That last point is the one that trips people up, so let's be honest about it up front.

The honest catch: it's paid in arrears

Grants are almost never a cheque you receive before you start. Made Smarter, like most of these programmes, reimburses against evidence of spend at agreed milestones. That means:

  • You need cashflow to cover the cost as it goes out; the grant is the refund, not the float.
  • The work gets phased so claims line up with milestones: mapping first, then the build, then go-live, with the claim attached to each stage.
  • Eligibility is the funder's call. Anyone who tells you a grant is "guaranteed" is either naive or selling you something; the decision sits with the programme, and the application is signed by the business owner.

None of this makes the grant worthless. It changes how you structure the project, which is exactly what a delivery partner is for.

What "digital work" means to a funder

Grants fund projects with outcomes, not "a new website". When a funder reads an application, they're looking for the work framed as things like:

  • Cutting manual work: quoting, order chasing, re-keying data
  • Opening a new sales channel or market
  • Innovating a product, service or way of working
  • Creating or safeguarding jobs

So the fundable shape is a project with work packages and measurable outcomes, not a line item. For a manufacturer that usually means three packages:

Work packageWhat it coversThe outcome a funder sees
Process mapping & roadmapHow quoting, orders and production data flow today, and the targetA scoped, costed project, not a vague "digitise us"
Systems buildERP/MRP or job-tracking selection & implementation, a customer portal for quotes/order statusLess admin, a new channel, fewer errors
Data capture & dashboardsOEE, lead times, defect trackingEvidence-based decisions, not notebook numbers

Each package has a cost and an outcome attached, so the budget maps cleanly onto grant categories and the claim process later.

A worked example for a fabricator

Say a sheet-metal fabricator wants a customer portal and a job-tracking system. The honest arithmetic:

  • Project cost: £20,000 (discovery, build, integrations, training, go-live)
  • Grant at 50% match: up to £10,000
  • Net cost to the business: £10,000

The ROI argument is what makes that £10,000 easy to say yes to. If manual quoting and order-chasing eats 10 hours a week at, say, £30/hour of owner time, that's £15,600 a year of admin cost alone, before you count the quotes lost to the firm that could respond in a day. The portal replaces "where's my job?" phone calls with a login. It also lets a larger customer check order status without ringing. At £15,600 a year of admin cost, the £10,000 net cost is repaid in under a year. That is what the match funding is for.

What it's OK to promise about a grant

After all the above, the three things a good partner will say clearly:

  1. "Based on similar projects this looks eligible for [scheme], which typically funds up to 50% of eligible costs." That is honest, specific, not a guarantee.
  2. "Grants are paid in arrears on milestones, so you'll need cashflow up front; we phase the work to match." That is the catch, said before you sign, not after.
  3. "We'll handle the digital and technical sections and the budget, but you'll provide the financials and sign the application as the owner." That is the division of labour, so nobody's surprised.

Any partner who can't say all three, or who promises the grant before the funder has looked at the project, is a reason to walk.
